A strong terminal return does not guarantee that an LP can meet every capital call along the way.

The general partner (GP) manages the fund and calls capital. The limited partner (LP) commits capital and must fund those calls. The fund then invests in portfolio companies. Our illustration tracks the LP’s funding resources, rather than a startup’s runway or the GP’s own cash balance.

We reorder the same 14 annual public-market returns, including one −40% interval, while preserving approximately 8% annual compounded performance. For the model’s fully public-funded $100m commitment, placing the loss in year 2 produces a $9.17m funding shortfall in year 4. Moving it to year 12 leaves every baseline call funded.

The boundary matters: the example starts with a $100m liquid budget and a $100m commitment, with no borrowing or outside top-ups. Other institutional assets and liabilities are excluded. Actual LPs may have other investments, income, credit facilities and more complex liquidity systems. A market drawdown does not automatically imply an institutional default.

Fund-level subscription facilities can alter call timing; LP-level financing provides resources to meet calls. Our model includes neither additional LP financing nor subscription-line changes to its stated schedule. Borrowing does not remove the underlying commitment obligation (ILPA, 2017).

These are original LVCS calculations under hypothetical assumptions, not estimates of institutional default probabilities or expected accessible VC returns. The broader commitment and liquidity questions are established in the literature (Gourier, Phalippou and Westerfield, 2024; Ang, Papanikolaou and Westerfield, 2014).
